In 1959 a Juno II was destroyed 93 seconds after liftoff because the propellant inside it was swinging back and forth. No engine failed. No structure cracked first. The liquid started sloshing, the rocket leaned to follow it, and the control system made it worse.

Most people picture rocket fuel as dead weight. A number on a mass budget that shrinks as you burn it. That model works fine right up until the tank is half empty, because a partially filled tank does not behave like a solid block at all. Some of the liquid is stuck to the walls and the bottom and rides along with the vehicle. The rest is free to run uphill on one side and downhill on the other, and that free portion swings with a rhythm of its own. On a big launch vehicle it can be tens of tons of liquid moving in a direction nobody commanded, at a rate that has nothing to do with what the engines are doing.

## The tank is a pendulum, and it retunes itself as it drains

The standard way to model this is not fluid dynamics. It is a pendulum. You take the sloshing portion of the liquid, replace it with a point mass on a hinged arm bolted inside the tank, and pick the arm length so the fake pendulum swings at the same rate the real liquid does. Everything downstream (loads, control stability, the actual flight software) is designed against that stick figure. It works because the first sloshing mode of a cylindrical tank really does behave like a rigid pendulum to a good approximation.

The rate it swings at is the **natural frequency**, the number of times per second the liquid completes a full back-and-forth cycle, measured in hertz (Hz). Two things set it. One is the acceleration pressing the liquid against the tank floor, which acts like gravity does for a real pendulum. More acceleration means a stiffer restoring push and a faster swing. The other is the tank diameter. A wider tank gives the wave a longer distance to travel, so it swings slower, exactly the way a longer pendulum ticks slower than a short one.

Here is the part I did not expect until I built it. After a rigid-body dynamics problem set I put the simplest possible version of this in MATLAB: a rigid rocket body with one pendulum inside it standing in for the liquid, using the standard cylindrical-tank slosh relation for a 3.7 meter tank. Then I swept the whole burn. I assumed the frequency would climb steadily, since acceleration climbs steadily as the vehicle sheds mass. It does not. At liftoff, around 1.2 g (g being one Earth gravity, about 9.8 meters per second squared), the slosh sits near 0.55 Hz. Acceleration builds, and by 3.5 g the slosh has climbed to about 0.94 Hz. But the tank is also emptying, and once the liquid gets shallow the depth term takes over and drags the frequency back down. Near depletion it falls to roughly 0.58 Hz. The curve goes up and then comes back down, which means the slosh frequency passes through the same band twice in one burn.

That is the whole problem in one sentence. A flight control system has a **bandwidth**, the range of frequencies it actually responds to, typically a fraction of a hertz up to a few hertz for a large launcher. The vehicle also has a first bending mode, the slow whip of the airframe flexing like a diving board, usually somewhere in the same neighborhood. The slosh does not sit still and let you design around it. It sweeps. If it parks on top of the control bandwidth, the sensors read the lean that the sloshing liquid caused, the computer gimbals the engines to correct, the gimbal shoves the tank sideways, and that shove is exactly the input that pumps more energy into the slosh. That is a positive feedback loop with propellant as the spring.

The fix is not clever software alone. It is metal. Rings called **baffles** are welded around the inside wall of the tank, flat annular shelves sticking into the liquid. They do not stop the wave. They force the flow to separate as it climbs past each ring, which turns organized swinging into turbulence and drains the energy as heat. Saturn I used radially segmented tanks and floating damper cans before the industry settled on plain ring baffles as good enough. They are not perfect: baffle damping depends on where the wave hits relative to the ring, so it is not uniform in every direction. And in the ugly case the liquid stops sloshing side to side and starts rotating around the tank instead, a stable spinning limit cycle that dumps torque into the roll axis, which is not a mode any linear model catches.

## Why this is a live problem in 2026, not a history lesson

SpaceX has the Starship Propellant Transfer Demonstration targeted for this year: two vehicles docked in orbit, one pumping propellant into the other. Slosh is named openly as one of the unsolved pieces, and the reason is the acceleration term. During that transfer the stack is under milli-g settling thrust, a tiny sustained push (thousandths of a g) whose only job is to hold the liquid at the bottom of the tank so the outlet gets liquid and not gas. Run the same slosh relation on a 9 meter Starship tank at that acceleration and the frequency collapses to roughly 0.01 Hz, a full swing every hundred seconds. It is slow, it is enormous, and it barely damps because there is almost no restoring force to work against. If it grows, liquid folds into the **ullage** (the pressurized gas filling the empty volume above the propellant), the gas condenses, tank pressure drops, and the transfer stops. The physics is the same as it was on Juno II. Only the acceleration changed, and that changed everything about the timescale.

The useful thing to carry out of this is that a rocket is not a rigid object with fuel in it. It is a rigid object structurally coupled to a large, slow, lightly damped oscillator that retunes itself continuously through the burn, and the flight computer has to stay out of resonance with a target that keeps moving. Every time you see a ring baffle in a cutaway tank diagram, that is not plumbing. That is somebody buying damping because software alone could not close the loop.
