A Canada goose weighs about as much as a bag of sugar. Hit one at cruise speed on approach and it can put a hole in a jet engine the size of a dinner plate. That's not a fragile engine. That's physics most people never stop to run.

The instinct is to picture a bird strike like a bug hitting a windshield: a smear, a mess, nothing structural. Engines are metal, birds are feathers and bone, so the outcome feels obvious. What actually happens is closer to a car crash than a bug splat, because the damage a bird strike does isn't about how heavy the bird is. It's about how fast it's going when it hits, and that speed matters far more than most people expect.

## The math behind the mess

Kinetic energy (the energy something carries because it's moving) scales with mass times velocity squared: KE = ½mv². The squared term is what makes bird strikes so much worse than their weight suggests. A bird that's twice as heavy hits twice as hard. A bird hit at twice the speed hits four times as hard. On approach and departure, jets are moving at 150 to 250 knots, which is fast enough to turn "mass times velocity squared" into a genuinely large number.

Here's the one I ran myself, because I didn't believe the comparison the first time I saw it. Take a 5 kg Canada goose (about 11 pounds) and a jet doing 275 km/h, a normal approach speed. Plug that into KE = ½mv² and you get roughly 14,700 joules. To put that in a unit that means something: dropping a 100 kg weight from 15 meters, roughly a five-story building, delivers about the same energy. That's the number I keep coming back to whenever someone calls a bird strike "just a bird." A goose at approach speed doesn't hit like a goose. It hits like construction equipment falling off a building.

Fan blades are built to handle a lot, but they're also built to be light, because every gram of extra metal spinning at 3,000+ rpm costs fuel efficiency for the entire life of the engine. So the blades are thin titanium or composite airfoils optimized for aerodynamic efficiency, not blunt-force resistance. When 14,700 joules of energy needs to go somewhere in a few milliseconds, it goes into bending, curling, or tearing that thin metal. And because the fan is spinning fast enough to complete a full rotation in a fraction of a second, one damaged blade can clip the blade behind it before the engine even has time to react. What starts as one bird can end as a cascading mechanical failure across the whole fan stage.

The engine's job at that point isn't to stop the damage. It's to contain it. Wrapped around the fan is a containment ring, a thick structural band of woven Kevlar or steel designed to catch a shattered blade before it exits the engine case at hundreds of miles an hour. That ring exists because of exactly the kind of energy calculation above: engineers assume a blade will eventually fail, whether from a bird strike or fatigue, and design the case around absorbing that specific amount of kinetic energy rather than hoping it never happens. It's the same logic as a car's crumple zone. You don't design against the crash never occurring, you design for what happens the moment it does.

## Why this is a certification problem, not a bad-luck problem

This is why bird ingestion isn't a maintenance footnote, it's a full FAA certification category under 14 CFR Part 33.76. Engine makers have to fire real birds, sometimes over a dozen per test, out of a gas-powered cannon into a running engine and prove it keeps producing usable thrust afterward. A single medium bird has to leave the engine capable of running for at least five minutes with less than 25% thrust loss. Small birds get hit repeatedly, up to 16 ingestions in one test sequence, and the engine still has to produce declining thrust for 20 minutes so the aircraft can land. None of that is arbitrary. It's reverse-engineered from exactly the energy math above, scaled to the worst credible bird your engine is likely to eat.

The reason this is back in the news is Jeju Air Flight 2216, which crashed in December 2024 after a bird strike damaged both engines on approach. The investigation found the crew shut down the less-damaged engine instead of the more severely hit one, turning a survivable bird strike into a fatal one. South Korea responded by mandating bird-detection radar and thermal cameras at every airport starting in 2026. In the US, the Aerospace Industries Association's Bird Ingestion Working Group recommended in April 2025 that the FAA toughen Part 33.76 specifically for larger birds and flocking strikes, the exact scenario that brought down Flight 2216. Regulators are catching up to something the energy math already told us: as air traffic grows and bird populations near airports don't shrink, the "worst credible bird" keeps getting bigger.

Once you've run the numbers yourself, you stop thinking of bird strikes as freak accidents and start seeing them as a known, quantifiable load case, the same way engineers think about turbulence or a hard landing. The engine isn't surviving bad luck. It's surviving a specific, calculated amount of energy that someone decided in advance it had to withstand, and that number is currently being revised upward.
