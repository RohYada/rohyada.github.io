Every landing, a 180,000-pound airplane comes down at a few feet per second and stops falling almost instantly. That energy has to go somewhere. It doesn't go into a spring bouncing the plane back into the air. It goes into heat, dumped into a leg of hydraulic fluid and nitrogen gas hidden inside the landing gear.

Most people assume landing gear works like a shock on a mountain bike: a spring that compresses on impact and pushes back. That's half right. There is a spring in there, in the form of compressed nitrogen gas. But a spring by itself is a bad shock absorber. It stores energy and gives essentially all of it back, which means an airplane landing on pure springs would compress on touchdown and then rebound, launching itself back into the air like a pogo stick. The other half of the system, the part that actually gets rid of the energy instead of just storing it, is a piston forcing oil through a small hole. That's the part doing the real work.

## Inside the oleo strut

The device is called an oleo strut (oleo just means oil), and it's a telescoping piston-and-cylinder assembly mounted between the wheel and the airframe. The upper chamber holds nitrogen gas, pressurized to somewhere around 1,000 to 1,500 psi even when the strut is fully extended and the airplane is sitting still. The lower chamber holds hydraulic oil. A tapered rod called a metering pin runs down the center, and as the strut compresses, the pin slides into an orifice, changing how much oil can squeeze past it at any given moment.

Here's the part that actually explains why this design works and a plain spring doesn't. As the wheel slams into the runway, the piston drives down, forcing oil through that narrowing orifice. The resistance this creates isn't constant. It scales with the square of how fast the oil is being forced through, so a small increase in impact speed produces a much larger jump in resisting force. That relationship comes straight out of orifice flow behavior (basically the same physics as sticking your thumb over a garden hose), and it's the key design trick: soft and forgiving on a gentle touchdown, aggressively stiff the instant the impact gets hard. The nitrogen above it does the opposite job. As the piston compresses that gas, its pressure climbs in a smooth, predictable curve, and that curve is what actually holds the airplane's weight once it's settled on the ground, the same way the air in a bike tire holds your weight once you stop moving. Separate the two jobs and the design makes sense: the gas is there to carry static weight and cushion small bumps, the oil is there to burn off the one-time hit of a touchdown.

I ran into the same shape of problem in a structures class, working strain-energy problems where you calculate how much energy an elastic member stores under load using U = ∫F dx, the area under the force-deflection curve. An ideal spring gives that whole area back. The moment I saw the actual numbers for a real landing, that stopped making sense as a design. A firm touchdown can dump energy comparable to dropping the entire loaded aircraft from 18 inches off the ground, and the gear has to absorb essentially all of it in roughly 150 to 300 milliseconds. Store energy like that in a spring and give it back and you don't get a smooth rollout, you get a bounce, then a wheel skipping across the runway right when you need it planted for braking. The oil metering is what converts that stored gas-spring energy into heat instead of handing it back, and it's why a well-designed oleo-pneumatic strut can absorb over 80 percent of landing energy compared to roughly 50 percent for a simple coil spring. That difference is also why the strut still has to let the piston rebound slowly on the way back up. As the gear extends after touchdown, the same metering pin controls oil flowing the other direction, so the airplane settles instead of springing back up onto its shocks.

## Why the whole system has to work together

None of this matters if the strut can't lock into position, which is exactly what exposed the stakes on June 4, 2026, when a Lufthansa 787-9 sitting at a gate in Frankfurt had its nose gear collapse underneath it. The aircraft wasn't landing. It was parked, being worked on for a landing gear door fault, and investigators found the nose gear downlock pin had been left out while the gear lever sat in the UP position. Without that pin holding the gear extended, nothing stopped the strut from folding, and the airplane's nose dropped onto the tarmac, injuring four people nearby. The oleo strut can be a perfectly engineered energy absorber and still be useless if the mechanical lock meant to hold it in place isn't there. Landing gear isn't one clever part, it's a stack of separate systems (the gas spring, the hydraulic damper, the metering pin geometry, the downlock) and a failure in any single layer removes the safety margin the others were counting on. Regulators size every layer around a worst case, a hard landing at max weight, so the strut and its lock carry margin most passengers never come close to using. The Frankfurt incident didn't happen because the strut failed to absorb energy. It happened because a completely separate layer, one with nothing to do with springs or damping, wasn't in place to begin with.

Next time you feel a landing that's smooth on a light touch and a lot firmer on a hard one, that's not luck or a good pilot alone. It's a piston pushing oil through a shrinking hole, converting your kinetic energy into heat instead of bouncing you back into the sky. The spring gets you the soft part. The damper is what keeps you on the ground.
