---
title: "Why the Skin Isn't What Holds the Wing Together"
description: 'The part of the wing everyone pictures barely does any of the structural work. A closed tube of spars and skin does the real job.'
pubDate: 'Jul 08 2026'
---

In 2010, Boeing engineers bent the wing of a 787 test airframe upward by 25 feet and it didn't break. That's not a fluke of composite material. That's a torsion box doing exactly what it was built to do.

Most people picture an airplane wing as a solid shape, maybe with some ribs inside for support, and the skin doing the heavy lifting. That's backwards. The skin on its own is thin sheet metal or composite laminate, a few millimeters thick, and it would buckle under real flight loads almost immediately. The actual load-bearing structure is hidden inside: two spars (the long beams running the length of the wing, front and back), connected top and bottom by the skin, forming a closed rectangular tube called the wing box. Everything else, the ribs, the stringers, the fairings, exists to keep that tube's shape and feed loads into it. The wing box is the wing. The rest is aerodynamic dressing.

## What a closed tube gets you that an open one doesn't

Here's the part that took me a full problem set to actually feel in my hands, not just accept from a formula. In Aerospace Structures, we ran torsion calculations on thin-walled sections, comparing a closed box to the same cross-section cut open into a C-shape. The difference in twisting stiffness wasn't 20% or 50%. It was closer to two orders of magnitude. Same material, same wall thickness, same overall shape. Cut one slit down the length of that tube and the torsional stiffness collapses.

The reason comes down to how shear stress (the internal force that resists one layer of material sliding past another) distributes itself. In a closed tube, when you twist it, shear flow (shear force per unit length around the cross-section) runs in a continuous loop all the way around, and every wall of the tube shares the twisting load. Cut the tube open and that loop breaks. The material near the cut can't transfer load across the gap, so the whole section has to resist twisting mostly by bending the thin walls locally instead of running a shear loop through them. That's a much weaker mechanism, and it's why an open channel section under torsion can twist visibly under loads a closed box shrugs off. This is the actual textbook result, called Bredt-Batho theory, and it's the reason wing designers go out of their way to keep the box closed. Every access panel and cutout in real wing skin gets reinforced around the edges specifically to restore continuity, because a big enough hole in the skin is a crack in that shear loop.

Once you see the wing this way, the rest of the internal structure makes sense as a division of labor. The spar caps (the thick flanges running along the top and bottom of each spar) are sized to carry bending load, the up-and-down force from lift pushing the wingtip skyward relative to the root. The skin and the spar webs (the vertical plates connecting the caps) are sized almost entirely for shear and torsion, the twisting load from lift acting ahead of or behind the wing's structural centerline, and from control surface deflections. Ribs aren't structural beams at all in the way people assume. They hold the cross-sectional shape so the skin doesn't buckle and so point loads, like an engine pylon or landing gear attachment, get spread into the box instead of punching through a local weak spot.

## Why this shows up in test videos, not just equations

This is why that 2010 Boeing test looked the way it did. Static test airframes get instrumented and loaded hydraulically at wing-mounted jacks until they reach 150% of the maximum load the aircraft is ever certified to see in service, called ultimate load. For the 787, that meant bending the wingtips up roughly 25 feet, a dramatic amount for something that looks rigid on the tarmac, without the structure failing. The wing box is designed to flex that much on purpose. A wing built stiff enough to not visibly bend would be needlessly heavy, and weight is the one thing every aircraft structure is fighting from day one. The box geometry is what lets engineers push deflection that far while keeping stress in the material within safe limits, because the closed section spreads the load so efficiently across the whole cross-section instead of concentrating it.

That torsion box design shows up everywhere structural engineers care about efficiency under twisting loads, not just wings. Helicopter tail booms, wind turbine blades, and even bicycle frames use closed box or tube sections for the same reason: shear flow needs a complete loop to work, and every open gap in that loop costs stiffness that has to be made up somewhere else, usually with more material and more weight.

The 787 test also mattered because composite wing boxes don't fail the way aluminum ones do. An aluminum wing gives visible warning before it breaks: rivets yield, skin wrinkles, the structure groans. A carbon fiber wing box can look fine right up until a bonded joint or a stack of plies delaminates (the layers separate internally, with no crack visible from outside) and loses stiffness in one localized spot. That's exactly what happened during an earlier stage of 787 testing, when the wing-to-body join began delaminating at the stringers just above limit load. Boeing reinforced the design and reran the test to 150% of ultimate load without further issues. The fix wasn't a redesign of the whole box, just reinforcement at the specific path where the shear loop had a weak link. That's the practical payoff of understanding shear flow as a loop rather than a vague property of "the wing": when something fails, you know exactly where to look.

Next time you're on a plane and the wingtip flexes on takeoff, that's not a structure barely holding together. That's a closed tube doing what an open one physically can't, moving exactly as much as its designers calculated it should.
