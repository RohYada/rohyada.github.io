On December 20, 2025, an Air France A320 climbing out toward cruise altitude heard a loud bang, saw flashes near the left engine, and felt the whole airframe shudder. The crew shut the engine down, declared an emergency, and landed safely in Lyon. What caused it has a name that sounds almost too simple: a compressor stall. The same thing that happens to a wing flying too slow or too steep happens to jet engine blades every day, just spinning at a few thousand RPM instead of flying through open air.

Most people assume a jet engine either works or it doesn't. But the compressor at the front of the engine is a stack of spinning airfoils (blades shaped like tiny wings) that behave exactly like the wing on the aircraft itself. Each stage of blades takes incoming air and squeezes it into a smaller space, raising its pressure before it hits the combustor. That only works if air flows smoothly over each blade. Change the angle that air hits the blade, or ask the compressor to push against too much pressure downstream, and the flow separates off the blade surface the same way it peels off a wing at high angle of attack. The compressor stalls.

## The mechanism: a wing's stall, happening in a spinning ring

Angle of attack (the angle between the oncoming air and the blade's chord line) is the variable that decides whether a wing generates lift cleanly or stalls. Push that angle too high and the air can no longer follow the curve of the surface. It separates, lift collapses, and drag spikes. In Aerodynamics lab, I plotted exactly this: lift coefficient against angle of attack for a wind tunnel airfoil, watching the curve climb in a straight line and then break sharply once the model passed its stall angle. That break is flow separation showing up as a number on a graph.

A compressor blade experiences the same angle of attack physics, but the airflow hitting it depends on engine speed, inlet conditions, and how much the stages behind it are already squeezing the air. Slam the throttle forward too fast, ingest a bird or a patch of turbulent air, or run the engine at low RPM with the nozzle set for high RPM, and the effective angle of attack on a ring of blades jumps past its stall point. Unlike a wing, where you can just push the nose down and unstall it, a compressor blade is stuck spinning at whatever the engine shaft speed set for it, so it can't easily reduce its own angle of attack once the separation starts.

There are two flavors of this failure. Rotating stall is the milder one: a pocket of separated, stagnant flow forms somewhere around the compressor's circular cross-section and rotates around the ring, degrading performance and shaking the blades without stopping the engine. It doesn't sit still because the blades on either side of the stalled patch see a slightly different angle of attack than normal, one gets pushed a little closer to its own stall point and the other a little further from it, so the dead patch effectively walks around the compressor at a fraction of shaft speed. Surge is the violent version. The compressor loses its ability to keep pushing air against the pressure already built up downstream, and instead of pressure rising smoothly stage by stage, the whole flow reverses for an instant and gets shoved back out the front of the engine. That's the loud bang and the flash of flame the Air France passengers saw: unburned fuel and hot gas getting expelled forward instead of flowing backward through the turbine like it's supposed to. A single surge event can last well under a second, but the pressure spike and the mechanical shock of the flow slamming back through the earlier compressor stages is enough to bend blades, which is why every reported stall gets the engine pulled for a borescope inspection even after a completely uneventful landing.

## Why it matters

Every modern engine control system exists partly to keep the compressor away from its stall line. FADEC (Full Authority Digital Engine Control, the computer that manages fuel flow and bleed valves) schedules fuel delivery so the compressor never gets asked to push harder than its blades can handle at a given speed, and bleed valves dump excess air overboard during rapid throttle changes specifically to prevent a stall from cascading into a surge. Some engines also use variable stator vanes, adjustable guide blades ahead of certain compressor stages, that physically rotate to change the angle air hits the rotating blades behind them, keeping the local angle of attack in a safe range across a wide range of engine speeds instead of just at one design point. Engineers design in a surge margin, extra distance between normal operating conditions and the stall line, the same way a wing's operating angle of attack is kept well below its stall angle during normal flight. That margin gets smaller as an engine ages, since worn seals let air leak past stages instead of being compressed, and a corroded or eroded blade tip changes the effective shape of the airfoil enough to shift its stall point. The Air France, Frontier, and Qantas incidents late last year all trace back to the same failure mode showing up in different circumstances, whether from a worn seal, a damaged blade, or an unusual flight condition that pushed local airflow past its limit.

Knowing this changes how you read those "engine failure" headlines. It's not usually a catastrophic mechanical break. It's the same aerodynamic stall you'd expect from a wing at too high an angle of attack, just happening to blades spinning inside a metal ring instead of a wing slicing through open sky, and the engine's own control software is built to keep it from ever reaching that point in normal operation.
